Research log · world models for robot manipulation

Fast, checkable and embodiment-agnostic world models

Every study we ran, with its hypothesis, design, setup, models and data, the numbers we got, and whether they support or contradict what we expected. That includes the ones that failed.

Last updated 13 October 2026 · 4 × RTX A6000 (48 GB) · 650+ GPU-hours of training and evaluation · ~1,000 world-model rollouts in simulation · 72 ContactGWM training runs

The work runs in three threads.

  • Thread A: making world action models fast. A world action model (WAM) controls a robot by imagining a short video of the future and the actions that go with it. LingBot-VA is accurate but needs 77 network passes per action chunk. Flash-WAM distils it to 4 passes with latent consistency distillation (LCM). We checked those claims, then replaced the LCM loss with Mean Flow Distillation (MFD) and asked whether a newer loss teaches a better student. Finally we asked whether the student's actions suffer because at deployment they condition on its own imperfect one-step video, tried a loss that targets that gap (Lcross), and trained a MeanFlow student with it.
  • Thread B: knowing when a world model is wrong. A WAM imagines before it acts. Can we look at what it imagined, alone or against reality, and tell whether the attempt will fail? We tried five signals: 3D cycle consistency (D4RT), cross-camera depth agreement (GeoBoN), gripper chatter, the gap between imagined and real video, and a learned predictor of that gap. We then used the predictor for best-of-N test-time scaling.
  • Thread C: ContactGWM. For an object-centric world model, is the trajectory of 3D contact points on the object surface a better action interface than end-effector pose or the actor's own geometry when the robot body changes? We tested this in simulation with a minimal world model, across 10 embodiments and 72 training runs.

Thread A · Making world action models fast

LingBot-VA starts each action chunk from noise and refines it over many denoising steps, which takes ~11 s per chunk on our GPUs. That is far too slow for a robot that has to react. Distillation trains a fast student to jump straight to where the slow teacher ends up. How the student is scored during training (the loss) is the variable studied in this thread.

A1 · LCM: replicating Flash-WAM

✓ Supported
Hypothesis

Flash-WAM's modality-aware LCM distillation makes LingBot-VA ~20× faster per chunk while keeping teacher-level task success, and the gain comes from distillation itself, not just from running fewer steps.

Research questions
  • RQ1 How large is the speed-up on our hardware (paper: 23.3×, 8.1 s → 348 ms on an L40S)?
  • RQ2 Does the low-NFE student keep the teacher's success rate?
  • RQ3 Does distillation beat simply running the teacher at the same low budget (1 video + 2 action steps)? The paper never tests this control.
Experimental design
  • Latency: per-chunk denoising time in bf16, 6 timed chunks after 2 warm-ups, single-rank FSDP, for every Nv/Na/CFG configuration.
  • Success: 3 RoboTwin tasks, one per horizon class, 10 trials each, under four conditions: teacher (25v/50a, 77 passes), teacher cut to 1v/2a (5 passes, no training, the control), Flash-WAM 1v/2a (5 passes), and Flash-WAM 1v/1a (4 passes). Same start seed (10000) in every condition, so trial i starts from the same scene.
  • Statistic for the key contrast: Fisher's exact test.
Setup

4 × RTX A6000 48 GB, driver 550, CUDA 12.4. Flash-WAM commit 5b8df13, RoboTwin 2.0 2eeec322. Model server: torch 2.9.0. Simulators: torch 2.4.1, in a separate environment.

Models & data

Teacher lingbot-va-posttrain-robotwin; student FlashWAM-RoboTwin (public). RoboTwin demo_clean: adjust_bottle (H1, 400-step cap), pick_dual_bottles (H2, 400), stack_blocks_three (H3, 1,200). No LIBERO student was released, so we trained our own with the same recipe (see A2).

A2 · MFD: swapping the distillation loss

✗ Refuted
Hypothesis

Replacing Flash-WAM's LCM loss with Mean Flow Distillation (MFD), with everything else fixed, gives a student that is at least as fast and at least as successful.

Research questions
  • RQ1 Can MFD's three networks (teacher, student, auxiliary flow) fit on 4 × 48 GB?
  • RQ2 Does the MFD student keep the low-NFE inference speed?
  • RQ3 How does task success compare with LCM on RoboTwin and on LIBERO-Long?
  • RQ4 What does MFD cost to train?
Experimental design
  • Same teacher, same full-size student, same data, same 2,000 steps. Only the loss differs. The original MFD release makes the student a LoRA; we deliberately kept a full student so that the comparison isolates the loss.
  • The auxiliary flow model is a LoRA on the frozen teacher (rank 64, α 64, 300 linear layers, 161 M params) that can be switched on and off. With adapters off, the module reproduces the teacher bit for bit.
  • Evaluation at 1v/2a, no guidance: RoboTwin demo_clean, 3 tasks × 10 trials; LIBERO-Long (libero_10), 10 tasks × 10 episodes = 100 per model (failed episodes hit the 796-frame cap).
Setup

FSDP with activation checkpointing on 4 GPUs, bf16, batch 1 per GPU, AdamW. MFD settings: ODE step 0.02, so ceil((t − r)/0.02) Euler substeps, ≈17 on average and up to 50; teacher CFG 4.5 at every substep; 50:20 auxiliary:student cycle as published, r ≠ t ratio 1.0 (a true mean flow), action target in x₀ mode, aux LR 5e-6.

Models & data

Teacher: LingBot-VA RoboTwin and LIBERO checkpoints. Data: robotwin-clean-and-aug-lerobot (1,000 episodes downloaded) and the LIBERO-Long post-training set (500 episodes). Students: libero_flashwam_full (LCM), libero_mfd_full and robotwin_mfd_full (MFD).

A3 · Why is MFD worse? Two explanations tested

✗ Both ruled out

MFD minimises gap, the RMS of uq − up (the teacher's mean flow minus the auxiliary model's estimate of the student's). It never shrank: it stayed flat at ~0.15 over 2,000 steps on LIBERO and drifted up, from 0.18 to 0.22, on RoboTwin. MFD trained without errors and produced a working policy, but it never closed the gap it exists to close.

Hypotheses
  • H-LR. The learning rate is too small. MFD student gradients had median norm 0.01, against 0.13 for LCM at the same LR.
  • H-sample. MFD learns from the wrong samples. Our port fed the student real training latents noised to a random level (often almost clean) instead of what the student produces at inference, i.e. one step from pure noise.
Research question

Does fixing either one make gap fall and held-out error improve?

Experimental design
  • One change per run, everything else as in A2: config_libero, 4 GPUs, the 50:20 cycle.
  • 500 steps per run = 140 student updates. That matches the original run's step-500 checkpoint, which was already on disk.
  • Scored on 50 unseen LIBERO episodes with fixed noise and timesteps (one-step x̂₀ MSE at σ ≈ 1).
  • Sample fix: the student gets pure noise at the scheduler's first timestep (σ = 1) with the clean history stream untouched (MFD_STUDENT_NOISE=inference).

A6 · How noisy is the video the action stream actually sees?

◐ Gap real; effect on actions unclear

At deployment the student's action stream does not see clean video. It reads the KV cache written by the student's own one-step video prediction. In training it always reads the clean ground-truth video. Before designing a loss for that mismatch, we measured how large it is and what the action stream can see during training.

Hypothesis

The student's one-step video sits measurably off the teacher's full rollout, at a noise level that training rarely visits, and the action stream's output is sensitive to that error.

Research questions
  • RQ1 How far is the student's one-step video from the teacher's 25-step video, expressed as an effective noise level σv,eff?
  • RQ2 How much of the video training schedule sits at or below that noise level?
  • RQ3 During training, can the action stream see the noisy video tokens at all? This decides how a corrective loss has to be built.
  • RQ4 Does the action stream's output react to the one-step video error?
Experimental design
  • σv,eff: paired rollouts with one shared noise sample per chunk. Teacher at 25 video steps with guidance 5; student at one step (σ = 1 → 0) with guidance 1 (deployed) and 5. The KV cache is driven by the teacher's outputs (teacher forcing), so each chunk is an independent paired measurement. σv,eff = RMS(x̂₀student − x̂₀teacher) / √(1 + σd²), on future video tokens only, with σd = 1.00 measured as the per-dimension std of clean latents (not the 0.5 constant in the LCM config).
  • Training share: the fraction of the video scheduler's training weight (shift 5) at σ ≤ the median σv,eff.
  • Action sensitivity: noise the teacher's clean video to σ ∈ [0, 0.5] (step 0.01) and find the σ at which the student's action output fa best matches its output under the student's own one-step video. Two estimators: the argmin of the distance, and a magnitude-matched variant. Actions are anchored on the teacher's own action output at action noise σa ∈ {0.1, 0.5}.
  • Reachability: rebuild the flex attention mask used in training (realistic RoboTwin shapes: 480 video + 64 action tokens, each as a noisy and a clean copy) and count attention edges between token groups. Then run a small model through the training forward twice, changing only the noisy video latents and their timesteps, and compare the action output under the flex mask and under unmasked attention.
Setup

Read-only measurements: no model or loss is modified. One GPU. 48 paired samples (24 demo_clean, 24 demo_randomized), 2 chunks each, seed 1234.

A7 · Lcross: training the action stream on its own imperfect video

✗ No significant gain
Hypothesis

Adding a cross-modal consistency term Lcross to the Flash-WAM loss closes the train/deploy conditioning gap found in A6 and raises task success at the same 1v/2a budget.

Method

Lcross = d( fastudent(aσ | degraded video) , sg[ faEMA(aσ | clean ground-truth video) ] ). Both sides see the same noisy action at the same σa; only the content of the video condition differs. The degraded condition is the EMA target's one-step video prediction x̂₀ = ε − v(ε, σ = 1). One extra no-grad EMA pass yields both that prediction and the clean-condition target, because the action head ignores the noisy video tokens (A6). Two alternatives are implemented: the student's own one-step prediction, and ground truth + Gaussian noise at σ = 0.127. d is the same masked pseudo-Huber as Laction.

  • RQ4 What does it cost?

  • Pairs trained from the teacher with identical data, seed and step count. Only Lcross differs: λc = 1.0, applied on 50% of micro-steps (pcross = 0.5), condition = EMA one-step prediction. Loss: Lvideo + 1.0·Laction + 0.01·Laa + λc·Lcross (the A1 recipe plus the new term).
  • Evaluation at 1v/2a with video guidance 5 and action guidance 1 (our runner's setting; A1 ran students without guidance), demo_clean, 10 episodes per task, same start seed. First the 6 distillation tasks, then a 25-task matrix: 10 H1 + 10 H2 + 5 H3 tasks, with horizon classes taken from Flash-WAM's per-task table. Statistic: Fisher's exact test.

With Lcross off, the random stream and the training step are unchanged from the Flash-WAM code by construction; a harness that diffs per-step losses and gradient hashes against the unmodified code is included.

A8 · MeanFlow + Lcross: an average-velocity target instead of LCM's

◐ +33 points · controls pending

LCM teaches the student a consistency map: from any noise level, land where the teacher's trajectory ends. A MeanFlow student instead predicts the average velocity u(zt, r, t) over an interval [r, t], so a single step from t to r is zr = zt − (t − r)·u. Thread B · Knowing when a world action model is wrong

A WAM imagines the future before it acts, so a natural idea is to read failure off what it imagines. If that worked, we could generate several candidate plans and keep the best one (test-time scaling), or stop an attempt that is going wrong. We tried five signals in sequence, and each failure shaped the next attempt. Signals B1–B3 inspect only the model's own output. B4 compares the output with reality. B5–B6 try to predict B4 ahead of time.

Two traps that shaped every analysis in this thread

1. Episode length leaks the label. RoboTwin stops a rollout the moment it succeeds, so failures always run to the step cap. In the first 150 rollouts, every failure lasted exactly 1,216 steps and every success 448–800. Any whole-episode statistic therefore scores AUC 1.000 by leakage. From B3 on, every score uses the same opening prefix for every rollout of a task.

2. Pooling across tasks fakes correlations. Tasks sit at different levels of every score, so a line drawn through task clusters can look significant when nothing exists within any task (Simpson's paradox). All tests below rank within task.

B2 · GeoBoN: do the imagined head and wrist views agree in 3D?

✗ Refuted
Hypothesis

From GeoBoN (arXiv 2607.17454): if the imagined head-camera and wrist-camera views describe the same 3D scene, the plan is good. Best-of-N selection by cross-view depth agreement raises success; the paper reports LIBERO-Long 97.2% → 98.3% with 8 candidates.

Research questions
  • RQ1 (validity) Can e_depth tell a matched head/wrist pair from a deliberately mismatched one (wrist view taken from a different rollout)? The paper never reports this check.
  • RQ2 Does it correlate with task success or with the denoising budget?
Score (Eq. 5)
e_depth = (1/|W|) · Σ_p | log( d_proj(p) / d_vggt(p) ) |
  d_proj = wrist depth, unprojected to 3D and reprojected into the head view
  d_vggt = the head view's own predicted depth at that pixel
  W      = pixels with positive depth and confidence > 0.5

The log ratio makes the score scale-free: a 20% depth error gives |log 1.2| = 0.182 at any scale. Both depth maps and the relative camera pose come from one joint VGGT-Ω pass over the (head, wrist) pair, so no calibration is needed.

Experimental design
  • No code was released, so we rebuilt GeoBoN from the paper: the evaluator (Eq. 5), the action–future agreement gate (Eqs. 2–4), the baseline / fixed-N / gated selector, and an N-way sampler with KV-cache rollback.
  • A synthetic self-check recovers e_depth = 3·10⁻⁸ on consistent geometry and 0.182 under a 20% depth error. The gate uses Farneback flow vs projected end-effector motion (τgate = −0.2, ρ = 30 px, δidle = 1 cm).
  • Discriminative-validity harness, run on LIBERO and RoboTwin before trusting the score: matched (head, wrist) pairs vs a time-shifted wrist frame vs a wrist frame from a different rollout.
  • Then scored the same 150 rollouts as B1.

B4 · The imagined-vs-real latent gap

✓ Confirmed (pre-registered)
Hypothesis

A rollout fails when the world does not do what the model expected. Encode, with the model's own Wan2.2 VAE, (a) the video the model imagined for a chunk and (b) the real frames recorded after its actions ran from the same state. The gap between the two latents is larger in failed rollouts than in successful ones, within task.

Research questions
  • RQ1 Is the gap a valid measurement: comparable latents, aligned chunks, not a proxy for motion or length?
  • RQ2 Does it separate failures within task, on fresh data, under a pre-registered test?
  • RQ3 When in a rollout does it separate: early enough to select plans, or only to monitor?
Score
gap_k   = mean over latent values of (imagined_k − real_k)²     # per chunk k
gap_mean = mean_k gap_k                                         # per rollout
variants: wrist cameras only; gap ÷ real scene motion ("skill")
Experimental design
  • A one-line model-server patch (VA_SAVE_REAL_LATENTS=1) saves the real latents during each rollout.
  • Discovery: 13 tasks × 6 rollouts (SEED 0), 21 failures. Seven variants were examined; only gap_mean had been defined before seeing data.
  • Pre-registration (committed 2026-10-02): 5 tasks × 14 fresh rollouts (SEED 1, start seed 20000). Primary gap_mean, confirmed iff within-task AUC > 0.5 and one-sided permutation p < 0.05 (10,000 permutations). Secondary wrist_mean and skill_mean at p < 0.025 (Bonferroni). The frozen analysis ran automatically once all 70 rollouts existed.
  • No task outside the training set exists (the checkpoint saw all 50 RoboTwin tasks), so low-success tasks are hard tasks inside it.

◐ Partly
Hypothesis

Research questions

  • RQ2 Does adding the imagined frame-to-frame difference ("imagined vs imagined") help?

Thread C · ContactGWM: contact as the action interface

Thesis. For an explicit, object-centric rigid-body world model, the trajectory of 3D contact points on the object surface is a sufficient and embodiment-agnostic action interface. It should beat end-effector pose (locked to one embodiment) and the actor's own surface geometry (which carries embodiment detail that does not matter to the object).

Design rule. Contact can fail as a representation (contact kinematics underdetermine the dynamics) or as an extraction (noisy contacts from video). The two must not be confounded, so everything here runs in simulation with one geometric contact operator shared by every embodiment. The state is held fixed as object surface points, deliberately without Gaussian splatting: splatting concerns the state representation, while the thesis is about the action interface.

C1 · Does a purely geometric contact operator match the physics engine?

✓ Supported
Hypothesis

One embodiment-agnostic geometric operator (object surface points within δ of the actor surface) recovers the contacts the physics engine reports, for any actor shape.

Research questions
  • RQ1 Precision and recall against the PhysX oracle as a function of δ.
  • RQ2 Which simulator backend exposes per-point oracle contacts?
  • RQ3 Is the operator analytically correct?
Operator

For each object surface point within δ of the actor it emits (position, flow, normal, separation, tangential velocity, confidence). Channel ladder: cf_min 7 = (x, y, z, Δx, Δy, Δz, w); cf_normal 10 = + normal; cf_sep 11 = + signed separation; cf_full 14 = + tangential velocity. The flow Δ is the actor's displacement, never the object's: object flow is exactly what the world model predicts, so conditioning on it would leak the answer. Object flow is kept only as a leak upper bound, and it raises an error unless allow_leak=True.

Experimental design
  • 14 analytic tests on sphere-on-plane geometry with closed-form answers.
  • A CPU-vs-GPU probe of the SAPIEN contact APIs, each backend in its own process.
  • Oracle validation: 5 YCB objects (cracker box, soup can, mustard bottle, banana, power drill), a kinematic sphere pusher, 5,093 true-contact frames, δ ∈ {2, 5, 10} mm.
Setup

ManiSkill3 / SAPIEN 3.0.3, torch 2.5.1 + cu124, 4 × A6000. SE(3) helpers round-trip to 1e-7; the geodesic angle uses atan2 in float64, because the naive acos form has a 0.04° float32 noise floor.

C2 · Which action interface transfers to unseen embodiments?

✓ Supported
Hypotheses
  • H1 (transfer) Conditioning on 3D contact gives lower prediction error on held-out embodiments than end-effector pose or the actor's surface geometry.
  • H2 (sufficiency) Adding end-effector pose on top of contact buys nothing.
  • H3 (normals) Surface normals help beyond the minimal contact flow.
Research questions
  • RQ1 Train on one embodiment (E1): which interface transfers best to 4 unseen ones?
  • RQ2 Train on three (E2): which interface beats constant-velocity extrapolation on 2 held-out embodiments?
Experimental design
  • One shared backbone with identical capacity, schedule and state tokens for every mode. Only the action tokens differ.
  • Two controls: none (no action input; a mode that cannot beat it contributes nothing) and constant-velocity extrapolation (momentum alone, with no model).
  • Metric: median position error of the composed 4-step prediction (cm) on action-critical chunks, where constant velocity misses by > 2 mm (21–31% of chunks). Averaged over all chunks, the coasting majority drowns the signal.
  • 3 seeds per cell; mean ± std across seeds.

C3 · Can you tell from the action alone whether the object will move?

✓ Supported (representation level)
Hypothesis

Near-misses (an actor passing close to the object without touching it) are where EE-pose conditioning should be weakest, because whether the object moves depends on geometry the pose does not encode. Contact makes hit-vs-miss linearly decodable.

Experimental design

From the action representation alone (no world model), linearly predict whether the object moves, on hard chunks where the actor is within 12 cm. 25% of episodes are planned near-misses.

C4 · The kill switch: does contact's advantage vanish as embodiments scale?

◐ Holds on action-critical chunks; decays elsewhere
Hypothesis (null to kill)

Contact's margin over actor geometry is an artefact of training on few embodiments. With more training diversity, actor-geometry conditioning learns to generalise, and the margin halves with each doubling of training embodiments. If so, the idea is not worth a paper.

Research question

How does the paired margin (actor_geom − cf_normal) / actor_geom on a fixed held-out set change as N grows from 1 to 8?

Experimental design
  • Five new embodiments built to span distinct contact-patch topologies, giving a pool of 10.
  • Nested training sets: {stick} ⊂ +sphere, panel ⊂ +roller ⊂ +fork, wedge ⊂ +probe, block (N = 1, 3, 4, 6, 8).
  • Held-out set fixed at {gripper, hand}. Total training steps fixed at 6,000, so the only variable is diversity (each embodiment gets 6,000 / N steps).

C5 · Novelty check and the GO / NO-GO review

◐ Conditional GO, reframed
Question

Is "3D contact as an embodiment-agnostic action interface" still an open claim, and is the project feasible on this hardware?

Method

A literature sweep against the MVE results, plus measured compute and toolchain costs on the 4 × A6000 machine.

FindingConsequence
DexWM (arXiv 2511.01177) represents hands and grippers as 3D particles and actions as end-effector particle displacement fields, i.e. our actor_geom baseline. μ₀ (arXiv 2606.13769) uses embodiment-agnostic 3D interaction traces.The original headline claim is no longer open. Conditional GO. The science works and is reproducible across seeds. The kill switch passed on the pre-specified metric (C4), which discharged the viability condition. What remains is framing (DexWM as the baseline; the less-is-more sufficiency claim) and depth beyond the interface comparison.

Lessons & silent bugs

What we would tell someone starting this work

  1. Compare the model with reality, not just with itself. Every score that looked only at what the model imagined (B1–B3) failed. The one that compared imagination with what actually happened (B4) worked.
  2. Judge a robot by what it does. MFD pictured the future more sharply and performed worse (A4). Action error tracked task success; video error did not.
  3. Simulators leak labels through episode length. Failed rollouts run to the step cap. Score the same prefix for every rollout.
  4. Decide the test before you see the data. Gripper chatter (0.79 → 0.60) and the latent gap (0.85 → 0.80) looked equally strong in discovery. Only a pre-registered test told them apart.
  5. Find the cheap baseline first. The teacher at 17 passes (A5), constant-velocity extrapolation (C2) and "little imagined change" (B5) were all baselines nobody reported, and each one reframed a result.
  6. Check what each stream can see before writing a loss for it. Our first cross-modal loss would have been a no-op: the training mask hides the noisy video from the action stream (A6).
  7. Label configurations by what actually ran. Several of our own logs called a 1v/2a run (5 passes) "4 passes". This page uses the true counts.
